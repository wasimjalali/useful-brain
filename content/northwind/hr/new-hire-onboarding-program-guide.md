# New Hire Onboarding Program Guide

## Program Overview


Northwind Systems operates a structured 90-day onboarding program for
every new employee, from individual contributors to managers and
directors. The program is built on one core principle: onboarding is the
hiring manager's responsibility, not HR's. HR provides the scaffolding,
the policies, the training content, the tracking tools, and the formal
checkpoints. But the hiring manager owns the outcome. This distinction
is critical because managers know what success looks like in their
teams, what skills are gaps, and what support their new hire actually
needs. HR ensures nothing falls through the cracks on the administrative
side, issues the security training, provisions the equipment, and flags
any risks such as extended delays in setup. But the manager drives the
actual ramp trajectory, schedules the one-to-ones, ensures the first
assignments are well-chosen, and advocates for the new hire if problems
arise.

The program is divided into three distinct phases, each with explicit
owners and deliverables. Week One focuses on access, equipment,
orientation, and compliance. IT owns hardware provisioning and account
creation. HR owns benefits enrollment, security and anti-harassment
training, and payroll setup. The hiring manager owns the first
one-to-one and a first project assignment. Weeks Two Through Four
emphasize role-specific ramp and integration. The hiring manager owns
the ramp plan, the 30-day check-in, and day-to-day mentorship. A peer
buddy assigned through the company program provides informal cultural
guidance outside the reporting line. By Day 90, the manager and HR
jointly assess readiness for full autonomy and formally close the
onboarding period. This checkpoint triggers the employee referral bonus
program for any employee who referred the new hire through the Recruiting
and Interview Policy.

Northwind operates with a distributed workforce across multiple office
locations and remote employees globally. Onboarding runs the
same way across all locations and for both fully remote and
office-based roles. The 90-day checkpoint is a company-wide milestone
that applies equally to individual contributors, managers, directors,
and executives. Exceptions or compressed timelines (such as for internal
transfers) require CHRO approval and are documented in the hiring
manager's file for audit purposes.

The program is not a one-way information dump. Hiring managers are
expected to seek feedback from IT, HR, and the peer buddy during each
phase, particularly if ramp is slower than expected. If a new hire is
struggling with systems mastery, cultural fit, or project velocity by
the 30-day mark, the manager escalates early to their own manager or to
the relevant functional VP (Chief Technology Officer for engineering, VP
Sales for sales, VP Customer Support for support, CFO for finance and
operations). This early escalation allows HR and the manager to adjust
the ramp plan, extend the onboarding timeline, offer additional
structured mentoring, or in rare cases, discuss a role fit concern
before the formal 90-day checkpoint. A 30-day or 60-day extension plan
negotiated openly is always preferable to a surprise assessment at day
90. The goal is for every new employee to have a fair, structured, and
supportive onboarding experience, and for every hiring manager and their
team to be intentional and prepared for a new person's arrival.

## Week One: Access, Equipment and Orientation


The first week of onboarding is the most administratively dense and most
critical. Success in Week One means the new hire has identity and access
provisioning complete, all required security and compliance training
finished, benefits and payroll set up correctly, equipment in hand, and
at least one meaningful conversation scheduled with their manager.
Failure in Week One compounds downstream. A laptop that arrives late
ruins productive work for the entire first week. Missed payroll setup
costs an employee money and erodes trust before they have begun. Skipped
security training leaves Northwind exposed to data loss or social
engineering. This section walks through each day of Week One, the
decision points that matter, and how to handle when things do not go
according to plan.

**Pre-start preparation: Three days before.** Preparation for a new hire
begins before Day One. The hiring manager ensures their team knows a new
person is joining, reserves desk space or confirms remote access setup,
and creates or assigns a first project for Week Two (see Weeks Two
Through Four for more detail on what that project should be). HR sends
the new hire a Welcome email confirming their start date, location,
reporting manager, and the hiring manager's contact information. That
email includes links to the pre-onboarding checklist, any paperwork to
sign electronically before arrival (tax forms W-4 or equivalent, NDA,
confidentiality agreement, emergency contact form). The Welcome email
notes that the new hire should bring a government-issued ID and their
Social Security number (or equivalent for international hires) for I-9
verification or local compliance requirements. The email should land at
least three business days before the start date to allow time for
electronic signatures and questions. For remote hires, HR provides a
link to the company handbook, benefits portal, and the Pulse workspace
setup instructions. If the new hire is based outside the United States
(London or Singapore), HR adjusts the compliance forms and tax
documentation requirements; I-9 does not apply outside the US and
different withholding forms are used.

**Day One morning: IT provisioning and network access.** IT provisions
all accounts before the new hire arrives at the office or logs in
remotely. This includes creating a network identity and email address
(format: firstname.lastname@northwind.example), setting up VPN access if
the new hire is remote, enrolling the new hire in the identity
management system, creating a Pulse account with appropriate channel
permissions, provisioning Northwind Core sandbox access (read-only by
default for non-engineering roles unless the role requires broader
access), Atlas demo account access, and Meridian customer portal read
access. IT also grants access to internal wikis, knowledge repositories,
the engineering repository (Codeforge for most teams, Relay for certain
infrastructure teams), and any project management tools designated by
the team's department for workflow tracking. IT batches all of these
into a single account-creation runbook so no account is missed and all
are ready simultaneously. The hiring manager receives a report (usually
by 10:00 CT) confirming which accounts are active and which are pending.
If any blocker exists at this stage (such as a name or email format
issue), it is escalated to HR, who contacts the new hire immediately to
correct it. The hiring manager is responsible for verifying that the new
hire has successfully logged into each system and can navigate to key
resources. Depending on the team's workflow preferences, the new hire may
be given access to project tracking through the tool designated by their
department.

**Day One morning: Hardware provisioning.** IT has already ordered and
staged a laptop, monitor, keyboard, mouse, and any role-specific
hardware (lab equipment, headset, docking station, second monitor for
certain roles). The laptop arrives preloaded
with the operating system, base software (Pulse, email client, VPN,
development tools if applicable), and security tooling (antivirus, disk
encryption, mobile device management agent). Before the new hire touches
the hardware, IT or the hiring manager prepares a form on paper or
digitally. The new hire signs the equipment issue and attestation form,
HR-114, which records the serial numbers of all hardware issued, the
condition upon receipt, and the new hire's agreement to return the
equipment in accordance with Northwind's Equipment Return Policy. A copy of HR-114 is
placed in the new hire's personnel file and a copy is retained by IT in
the asset tracking system. HR-114 is the formal record against which IT
audits equipment at departure. If HR-114 is not signed by the end of Day
One, the hiring manager and HR escalate this immediately because an
unsigned form creates a liability gap: if the employee later claims they
were not told about their equipment responsibility or leaves without
returning hardware, an unsigned HR-114 weakens Northwind's position. If
HR-114 remains unsigned by end of Day Two, the employee cannot access
production systems until the form is retroactively signed. All equipment
leaves the desk with a tamper-evident asset tag. The hiring manager
verifies the new hire has logged in successfully to the laptop, updated
their email password, confirmed Pulse works, and tested VPN access if
remote. IT confirms multi-factor authentication (MFA) is enabled and
working. Any network access issues or firewall exceptions are tested
live with the new hire at their desk or screen-share.

**Day One afternoon: HR orientation and initial setup.** HR conducts a
structured 60- to 90-minute orientation session with the new hire,
either in a group if multiple people are starting, or one-on-one. The
session covers company history, the distributed office and remote work
structure, the
three main products (Northwind Core for workflow orchestration, Atlas
for data integration, Meridian for customer portal access), and key
corporate policies. Policies covered include confidentiality and
intellectual property, code of conduct (respectful collaboration, no
discrimination or harassment, professional communication),
anti-harassment and discrimination, remote work norms, and the expense
reimbursement process. HR provides a printed handbook and a digital
copy, plus a quick-start guide to internal tools (Pulse workspace
navigation, the internal wiki for policy and process docs, the expense
system, the calendar and time-off tool, the benefits portal). The new
hire is given contact information for HR support (email and Pulse
channel) in case questions arise after this session. By end of Day One,
the new hire should understand where to find answers to most
administrative questions and should feel welcomed.

**Day One evening: Direct deposit setup.** Before close of business on
Day One, HR sends the new hire a secure link to enter banking
information for direct deposit (never via email or an unsecured form).
The new hire enters their bank account number, routing number, and
account type (checking or savings). This information is entered into the
payroll system immediately so that payroll deductions and the first
paycheck can be processed on the standard schedule. Any delays in this
step (such as a new hire not completing it until Day Two afternoon)
delay the first paycheck by one pay cycle. HR emphasizes the urgency and
includes a note in the email that completed forms must be received by
end of business on Day One.

**Day Two morning: Security and compliance training.** The new hire
completes mandatory security training within the first two business
days, ideally Day Two morning. The training is a 45-minute online module
covering data classification (public, internal, confidential,
restricted), password security (length, uniqueness, storage, never
sharing), phishing awareness and reporting, acceptable use of company
equipment and networks, and incident response basics (who to contact if
a device is lost or if an email phishing attempt is detected). The
module includes a final quiz with a passing score requirement of 80%. If
a new hire scores below 80 on the first attempt, they receive a
follow-up message with resources and may retake within one business day.
Failure to complete the training within the first two business days is
escalated immediately to the hiring manager and Chief HR Officer. On Day
Two afternoon or Day Three morning, the new hire completes
anti-harassment and discrimination training, which covers Northwind's
zero-tolerance policy, how to report concerns confidentially through HR
or an external ethics hotline, and the company's commitment to a
respectful workplace. Both trainings are required for all employees and
contractors before they can access production systems or customer data.
Training completion is logged in the Learning Management System and
verified by HR. The CHRO's office conducts a monthly audit of completion
rates to ensure accountability.

**Day Two afternoon: Benefits enrollment.** HR schedules a structured
60- to 90-minute session with the new hire to complete benefits
enrollment. The HR benefits coordinator walks through each benefit
option in detail: medical plans (options, deductibles, out-of-pocket
maximums, network coverage in the employee's location), dental and
vision, life insurance (company-paid base coverage plus voluntary
supplements), health savings account (HSA) if eligible, flexible
spending account (FSA) for dependent care, commuter benefits (transit or
parking subsidies if the employee commutes to an office), and retirement
and financial wellness programs. For international employees,
local benefits are handled by the regional HR contact with equivalent
coverage. The coordinator explains employee contribution rates (the
amount taken from each paycheck), when coverage becomes effective
(usually the first of the month following the start date), how to use
each benefit after enrollment, and how to contact the carrier's support
line with questions. The new hire makes elections during the session and
the coordinator enters them into the benefits administration system
immediately so that payroll can apply deductions starting the next
payroll cycle. If a new hire has existing coverage through a spouse's
plan, declines certain benefits, or has other special circumstances,
those elections are documented and filed. The session ends with the new
hire understanding their monthly premium amounts, their out-of-pocket
medical costs, which providers or facilities are in network, when each
coverage becomes effective, and how to access the benefits portal for
future changes.

**Day Two to Wednesday: Payroll tax setup.** HR finalizes payroll setup
by Day Three. This includes confirming the new hire's tax withholding
(W-4 form or equivalent, filled out or emailed back on the first day),
inputting start date, salary or hourly rate, position level and title,
department, cost center, and any special designations such as contractor
status, visa sponsorship (if applicable), on-call rotation membership,
or participation in equity compensation. The payroll processor validates
all of this data before the next payroll cycle so the new hire receives
their first paycheck on the standard schedule (bi-weekly on Friday
afternoons in the US; weekly or monthly varies by country for
international employees). Any delays in payroll data entry (such as a
banking form submitted late on Wednesday) delay the first paycheck by
one full pay cycle, which can create genuine hardship for an employee
relocating for the job. HR flags any incomplete data immediately and
contacts the new hire by phone if email follow-up has not worked.

**Day Three: Location-specific logistics.** For Austin-based employees,
the office manager or a designated team member schedules a tour of
headquarters (building access and keycard provisioning, parking,
restrooms, the kitchen and break areas, emergency exits and procedures,
the all-hands meeting space, the wellness facilities if any). For
London-based employees, similar logistics apply to the London satellite
office. For Singapore-based employees, a similar tour is scheduled,
typically in the afternoon Singapore time so the hiring manager can join
remotely if they are in Austin. For fully remote employees, the office
manager or hiring manager sends a link to a video walkthrough of the
relevant office if one exists, or simply confirms the location and
directions to Northwind facilities in case the remote employee visits
later for meetings or events. All new hires, regardless of location,
receive a list of recommended restaurants, gyms, public transit
information, or other local resources relevant to their office or area,
compiled by the office manager. This gesture, small as it is, reinforces
that Northwind cares about the employee's full experience.

**Day Three: First manager one-to-one.** By end of Day Three, the hiring
manager schedules and conducts a formal first one-to-one with the new
hire. This is a 30- to 45-minute calendar block, not a drive-by chat in
the hallway. The manager confirms the new hire has received all
materials and has successfully set up their equipment. The manager then
discusses the role in detail: the responsibilities, the team structure,
the immediate priorities for Week Two and beyond. The manager reviews
the 30-day goals (a role-level target agreed with their own manager
before hire day as a baseline), gives the new hire a copy of the company
handbook, reviews key policies (time off guidelines, expense
reimbursement process, remote work norms, code of conduct), and answers
initial questions. This is the moment when a manager sets tone: a
manager who is disorganized or dismissive in the first one-to-one
signals to the new hire that excellence is not a priority. A manager who
is prepared, engaged, and responsive signals the opposite. By end of
this conversation, the manager and new hire should have scheduled a
recurring weekly one-to-one time for at least the first 90 days.

**Handling equipment delays.** If equipment is delayed (laptop not
available, monitor on backorder, special peripherals ordered from an
external vendor), the hiring manager and IT work together to get the new
hire productive with what is available. A development engineer might
borrow a loaner laptop from IT, work on non-sensitive tasks on their
personal machine, or use pairing sessions with existing team members to
unblock early learning. A sales employee might start with CRM training,
competitor research, or customer background preparation while awaiting
their laptop. The key principle is that delay is not an excuse for
passivity or a slow start to the new hire's first week. If equipment is
still not available by end of Friday (Day Five), the hiring manager
escalates immediately to the department VP and HR to understand the root
cause and expected resolution date. If a monitor or keyboard is missing
but the laptop works, that is acceptable and the missing item can arrive
early the following week. But if the laptop itself is delayed past Day
Five, this is a serious problem and requires a shipping expedite or a
loaner device from IT's spare pool. The goal is for the new hire to have
at least a functioning computer by Day Two to start exploring internal
systems and completed training.

## Weeks Two Through Four: Role Ramp


The second phase of onboarding is role-specific and manager-led. The
hiring manager designs a ramp plan tailored to the new hire's
background, the role's requirements, and the team's current priorities.
The goal is for the new hire to ship or own something real by the end of
Week Two, to take autonomous ownership of a small project by the end of
Week Three, and to operate semi-independently by Week Four. The 30-day
conversation (scheduled for Day 30, not Day 25) is the first formal
checkpoint: Has ramp progressed on track? Are there gaps? Does the new
hire need more support or a different kind of support? Ramp speed varies
significantly by role and background. An engineer joining from a similar
technology stack ramps much faster than someone moving to a new language
or architecture. A customer support specialist ramping into Northwind's
products moves at a different pace than someone with five years of
similar support experience in similar enterprise software. A sales
representative with SaaS background ramps faster than someone from
inside sales or field service. The manager should account for this
variance and avoid applying a formula uniformly. This section walks
through role-specific ramp for each major function and highlights common
patterns across all roles.

Every new hire in every role is assigned a peer buddy through
Northwind's NW-BUDDY program. The NW-BUDDY program matches each new hire
with an experienced mentor from outside their direct reporting line for
the first eight weeks of employment. The buddy is explicitly not the
hiring manager, is not involved in performance evaluation, and is not
accountable for the new hire's work output or delivery timeline.
Instead, the buddy serves as an informal guide to unwritten knowledge:
they answer questions about how things actually get done (as opposed to
how they are supposed to get done), they introduce the new hire to
people across teams who might otherwise stay invisible on the org chart,
they give the new hire a safe space to ask questions that feel too
junior or embarrassing to ask the manager, and they help the new hire
understand the unwritten culture, politics, and norms. A good buddy
relationship opens doors and dramatically accelerates cultural
integration. A buddy who is too busy or unengaged means the new hire
figures things out the hard way through trial and error. Buddy matching
is handled by HR and the hiring manager in tandem. HR maintains a
voluntary NW-BUDDY roster of employees who have expressed interest in
mentoring and who have successfully completed the buddy training
program. The hiring manager provides context on what kind of peer would
be most helpful given the new hire's background (someone from
engineering, someone who has been at Northwind for 2+ years, someone who
has worked with the customer base, someone on the same location or
timezone). HR reviews candidates against this profile and makes a match.
The match is confirmed by Friday of the week before the new hire starts,
so a kickoff one-to-one with the buddy can happen on Day Two or Three.
Buddy responsibilities end after eight weeks. If the new hire and buddy
want to continue the relationship informally, that is up to them, but
the formal program obligation is complete at Week Eight.

**Engineering ramp.** The engineering ramp begins with system setup and
environment provisioning. The new engineer receives read-only access to
all repositories, the development and staging databases (with read-only
access at first), and Northwind Core in a sandbox account with limited
permissions. They are added to all relevant engineering Pulse channels
(general, #engineering, any guild channels like #frontend-guild or
#infrastructure-guild, the on-call channel if applicable). On Day Two or
Three, a senior engineer or tech lead walks the new engineer through
development environment setup in detail: cloning the main repository,
installing dependencies, running the full test suite locally (confirming
that all tests pass in the baseline), building the application locally,
running the local development server, and navigating the core
directories and files. This session typically takes 90 to 120 minutes
and is a great opportunity for the new engineer to surface environment
setup bugs or missing documentation that have crept in over time. By end
of Week One, the new engineer has run the full test suite locally and
deployed a change to the development environment (even if it is just a
documentation fix or a trivial refactor). This proves the development
environment works end-to-end and gives the engineer confidence in their
setup. In Week Two, the engineer is assigned their first real project:
usually a small, scoped bug fix or a limited feature that involves
touching multiple parts of the system but has very clear scope and
acceptance criteria. This project is worked on in close pairing with a
senior engineer or with frequent code review cycles. The goal is for the
new engineer to see the full development workflow: writing code, running
the test suite, pushing to a branch, creating a pull request, receiving
detailed review feedback, addressing feedback, and finally deploying to
staging. By the end of Week Two, this first project ships to production.
It is small and scoped, but it is real code that customers will use. In
Week Three, the engineer takes on a slightly larger project, often
working more independently with daily or twice-weekly check-ins rather
than pairing. By Week Four, the engineer understands the main codebase
architecture, has deployed multiple changes, has received feedback on
code quality and style, and is beginning to pick their own projects from
the backlog with the manager's input. The 30-day conversation focuses
specifically on code quality feedback from senior reviewers, progress on
system mastery (especially understanding the payment system, customer
data model, or whatever subsystem is most critical), whether the
engineer feels they grasp the architecture, and what resources would
accelerate the ramp (additional pairing with a specialist, time to
refactor a confusing area, training on a specific framework or tool).

**Sales ramp.** Sales ramp emphasizes customer access and deal flow
learning. The new sales representative is assigned a book of accounts on
Day One (a portfolio of existing customers or target accounts, drawn
from the broader territory or segment the new rep owns). In Week One,
they shadow at least two customer calls or meetings (live video, phone,
or in-person, not just recording playback) and attend a comprehensive
internal product overview covering Northwind Core in detail (key
features, limitations, roadmap), Atlas data integration capabilities,
Meridian customer portal, the competitive landscape (who the main
competitors are, how Northwind compares on price and features, common
customer tradeoffs), recent customer wins (case studies or anonymized
examples), and common customer objections and how the sales team
typically addresses them. By end of Week One, the new rep has reached
out to at least five customers in their book (via email, message, or
other communication channels supported by the company's outreach tools)
to introduce themselves and suggest a brief call to learn the
customer's current priorities and challenges. In Week Two, the new rep
books and runs their first customer call, usually with a prospect
already on the pipeline or a customer up for renewal conversation. The
manager or a senior rep sits in as a co-presenter, takes notes, and
provides signal on the call's quality. After the call, there is a
structured debrief: what went well, what was awkward or unclear, what
objection handling would the rep do differently next time, what did the
customer care about most. In Week Three, the new rep starts
participating actively in deal work. They prepare customer presentation
decks, take structured notes on calls, help the manager build a
discovery agenda for a key opportunity, and begin to build their own
outbound flow (reaching out to inbound leads or reactivating past
contacts). By end of Week Three, the new rep has scheduled at least two
independent discovery calls (the manager or buddy might still attend for
coaching, but the rep is driving). By end of Week Four, the new rep has
run at least one fully independent call with an existing customer and
one with a prospect, and they are on track to own a portion of the
pipeline. The 30-day conversation covers deal workflow understanding
(how long cycles are, who the decision-makers typically are, what the
contracting process requires), comfort with objection handling and
discovery methodology, internal stakeholder relationships (getting
timely support from the solutions team, engineering responsiveness, CFO
buy-in for large deals), and support the rep needs from the manager or
peer buddy.

**Support ramp.** Support ramp centers on ticket handling, customer
communication, and internal escalation paths. The new support
representative is added to the support queue on Day One, but only as an
observer initially (they read recent tickets, watch how senior reps
respond to customer questions, see how tickets are categorized and
prioritized, observe escalation paths). In Week One, they attend a
comprehensive product walkthrough (same as sales, but support-focused:
how to navigate Northwind Core, common user errors and how to resolve
them, the parts of Atlas that customers get stuck on, Meridian
limitations and documentation). They also receive a detailed briefing on
support SLA policy (P0 / P1 / P2 / P3 response and resolution times,
24/7 coverage for P0 and P1 during business hours and after hours, 8:00
to 18:00 CT coverage for P2 and P3, 4-hour response time for P2 during
business hours). They learn the ticketing system workflows, how to
search the knowledge base and prior tickets for answers, how to escalate
to engineering or the solutions architect when needed, and how to handle
billing or account questions. By end of Week One, the new rep has
responded to at least three low-priority tickets (P3, for non-critical
customer issues) with manager review before every customer response is
sent. In Week Two, the rep takes on P3 and emerging P2 tickets
independently but with same-day review from a senior rep, who checks for
tone, accuracy, whether the issue was actually resolved or just patched.
The manager observes the rep's responses looking for signs of empathy
and customer-centricity, whether escalations are appropriate, and
whether the rep is building systematic problem-solving skills or just
answering one-off questions. In Week Three, the rep begins P1 ticket
work, but only during business hours and with escalation support
available (a senior rep is on standby if the new rep is stuck or needs a
second opinion). In Week Four, the rep is fully in the on-call rotation
(taking pages outside business hours if P0 or P1 escalates) with
escalation support from senior reps. The 30-day conversation evaluates
customer feedback scores (CSAT ratings or NPS comments), ticket
resolution rate (percentage of first-contact resolution vs. tickets that
require follow-up), whether the rep is empathetic and accurate, whether
they are escalating appropriately or escalating too much, and what
product or process training gaps remain (deep knowledge of certain
features, handling particularly difficult customers, time management for
high-volume days).

**Finance and operations ramp.** Finance and operations ramp involves
process mastery and internal stakeholder mapping. The new finance or
operations hire is walked through every system they will touch: the
accounting system (for finance, including AR/AP, GL, journal entries),
the billing platform (customer invoicing, subscription management,
amendment processing), the procurement portal (purchase orders, vendor
management, three-way matching), the expense system (employee and
contractor reimbursement workflows), the payroll system (if operations),
and any custom finance or operations dashboards (cash flow forecasting,
headcount planning, budget vs. actual tracking). In Week One, they
shadow at least two team members working through their daily workflows:
recording customer revenue transactions, processing vendor invoices,
reconciling credit card charges, handling an employee expense report,
placing a purchase order. They also meet with leaders from other
departments (engineering, sales, support, people) to understand their
finance and operations touch-points (how engineering orders equipment,
how sales processes customer discounts, how support handles customer
credits, how people manage benefits and payroll). By end of Week One,
the new hire has run a sample workflow end-to-end under guidance: record
a sample revenue transaction, process a vendor invoice, reconcile an
account, place a purchase order, or handle an expense reimbursement. In
Week Two, they own a portion of the team's workload independently. A
finance hire might handle vendor invoice processing, customer invoice
review, or accounts reconciliation. An operations hire might own
procurement for a specific set of vendors, handle benefits
administration questions, or manage office logistics. The manager
reviews their work daily in Week Two, every other day in Week Three, and
weekly by Week Four. In Week Three, they participate in a monthly
financial close or payroll cycle, seeing how the detailed work they have
done fits into the larger financial picture and seeing where controls
and reconciliation points are critical. By end of Week Four, they
understand the full month-end and quarter-end close processes (what
happens on the last business day of the month, what reconciliations are
required, what gets reported to the board) and have contributed to at
least one close cycle. The 30-day conversation assesses accuracy and
attention to detail, understanding of control points and reconciliation
(why certain checks exist, what happens if a reconciliation does not
balance), and whether they feel confident in the systems and processes
or whether they still need significant guidance.

**The 30-day check-in conversation.** The 30-day conversation is not a
formal performance review in the HR sense, but it is a structured
checkpoint with clear outcomes. The manager prepares by gathering
feedback from senior team members who have worked with the new hire over
the past month, reviewing any work the new hire has shipped or
completed, and noting any gaps or concerns. The new hire prepares by
reflecting on what they have learned, what has been harder or easier
than expected, and what support would help them accelerate. The
conversation includes specific feedback on the new hire's work quality
and pace, discussion of what comes next in the ramp plan (priorities for
Weeks Five through Eight), and clear input from the manager on what is
going well and where more effort is needed. If ramp is on track, the
message is: "You are tracking well, here is what is next, I have
confidence in you." If ramp is behind, the message is concrete and
specific: "This area is not yet where I need it to be by Day 90. Here is
what needs to change by Day 60, and here is what I will do to support
you." Both conversations end with scheduling the Day 60 check-in and
confirming the Day 90 checkpoint date.

**Signals that ramp is going wrong.** Warning signs that ramp is off
track are concrete and should trigger immediate escalation to HR and the
department VP. In engineering: the new engineer has not shipped any code
to production by Week Three, or shipped code with numerous defects or
architectural misunderstandings. In sales: the new rep has not booked
any customer calls by end of Week Two, or calls have been poorly
prepared (rambling, unclear on customer needs, no agenda). In support:
tickets have recurring factual errors or incomplete investigations;
customer satisfaction is declining compared to team baseline; the rep
seems to be escalating everything rather than developing judgment on
what can be resolved directly. In finance and operations: work has
required significant rework or correction by a senior team member;
reconciliations consistently do not balance; accounting entries have
errors; the hire seems lost in the systems and remains dependent on
step-by-step guidance. These are not personality issues or cultural fit
concerns yet; they are skill, background, or role alignment concerns,
and they need to be addressed directly and quickly. If a new hire is
visibly behind at the 30-day mark, the manager escalates to the
department VP and HR immediately to develop a recovery plan. Options
include: extending the ramp timeline (moving the 90-day checkpoint to
Day 120), adding structured mentoring or pair programming (particularly
in engineering), offering technical training or bringing in a
specialist, or discussing whether there is a role fit issue that
warrants a conversation about a different role or a revised scope. None
of these options are shameful or a reflection on the employee as a
person. But ignoring a signal and hoping the new hire catches up by Day
90 is a manager mistake and sets everyone up for disappointment.

## The 90-Day Checkpoint


The 90-day checkpoint is a formal gate and milestone. It marks the end
of the onboarding period and
sets the baseline for ongoing performance expectations. For employees
referred through the Recruiting and Interview Policy, this checkpoint also
triggers the employee referral bonus program. For most
employees, the checkpoint is also the first formal review cycle
touchpoint, though reviews are administratively separate from
onboarding. The checkpoint is not a pass-fail exam that ends employment
if the outcome is not green. It is a calibration point that asks: Is
this person ready for full autonomy and independent project ownership?
Are there residual gaps that warrant extended support or a different
role? Is there a performance issue that warrants a Performance
Improvement Plan or further discussion? The checkpoint is a shared
assessment by the manager and HR, and it is never a surprise to the new
hire.

**Who conducts the assessment.** The hiring manager is responsible for
the factual assessment of readiness. Starting at Day 60, the manager
compiles observations across the 90 days: specific pieces of work
completed by the new hire, the quality of that work as reflected in peer
code review (for engineers), customer feedback (for sales and support),
or reconciliation accuracy (for finance and operations). The manager
gathers structured feedback from senior team members who have worked
with the new hire, from the peer buddy, and from other departments if
relevant (engineering feedback on a sales rep's technical understanding,
support feedback on a new engineer's deployment communication). The
manager documents their own judgment of whether the hire has met the
three core criteria for onboarding completion. HR reviews the manager's
assessment and confirms that all onboarding administrative items have
been completed (security training, anti-harassment training, benefits
enrollment, payroll setup, equipment signed for via HR-114, 30-day
check-in documented). HR also flags any red flags or concerns from the
onboarding period (unexplained absences, conflicts, incomplete training
by deadline). The Chief HR Officer's office reviews all checkpoint
assessments for consistency across the company and to ensure standards
are applied uniformly. For people leaders (hiring managers, directors,
VPs), the CHRO conducts or directly participates in the assessment to
ensure alignment with leadership expectations and company culture.

**The three core assessment criteria.** Competence is role-specific and
should be measured against Day-One expectations, not against a senior
practitioner's performance. A software engineer demonstrates competence
by writing code that passes peer review, deploying to production with
appropriate testing, and showing understanding of the codebase's
architecture. They do not need to be productive at the speed of a 5-year
engineer. A sales representative demonstrates competence by running a
structured discovery call, qualifying a lead against the ICP,
understanding the deal process, and executing an outbound cadence. A
customer support specialist demonstrates competence by resolving
straightforward tickets independently, escalating appropriately to
engineering or a solutions architect, and handling difficult customer
interactions professionally. A finance employee demonstrates competence
by processing transactions accurately, reconciling accounts without
error, and understanding the reason controls exist. Competence does not
mean mastery or production-level velocity; it means the hire can perform
the core job function without constant supervision or rework. The second
criterion is understanding of Northwind culture and norms. The hire has
attended team meetings, participated in social events or team lunches,
engaged in Pulse conversations, and can articulate Northwind's values
(customer obsession, data-driven decision-making, collaborative problem
solving, bias to action) and work norms (async documentation, meeting
efficiency, code review expectations, respecting time zones). The third
criterion is functional independence. The hire can set their own agenda
in consultation with the manager, can request feedback and support when
needed without excessive hand-holding, and is taking ownership of their
work and decisions. They do not need to be liked by everyone or to have
bonded deeply with peers, but they need to be functioning as a peer and
colleague, not as a dependent.

**Timeline and checkpoint documentation.** The manager begins compiling
observations formally at Day 60 during the Day 60 check-in conversation.
At Day 85, the manager sends a written summary to HR: typically a one-
to two-page document listing key accomplishments and projects completed,
specific feedback from peers or customers (quantified if possible: "code
review feedback was consistently positive," "customer CSAT on tickets
was 8.5 out of 10," "reconciliations have been error-free"), learning
progress (what areas did they develop mastery in, what areas are still
developing), and the manager's preliminary readiness assessment (green,
yellow with specific concerns, or red with specific reasons). HR reviews
this summary for completeness and flags any missing onboarding items
(incomplete training beyond Day Five, benefits enrollment not finalized,
HR-114 not signed, payroll not set up, 30-day check-in not documented).
At Day 89, the manager and new hire meet for the formal checkpoint
conversation. The manager presents the summary, shares specific examples
and feedback, and asks if the hire agrees with the assessment and feels
the summary is fair. This is a dialogue and discussion, not a one-way
presentation. If the hire disagrees or has concerns about the
assessment, those concerns are documented and considered by the manager
and HR. By end of Day 90, the manager and HR jointly complete and sign
the checkpoint confirmation record, form HR-208. This form records the
new hire's name, position, start date, hiring manager name, the
checkpoint outcome (green, yellow with reason and revised checkpoint
date, or red with reason), any special circumstances (the new hire was
on vacation during a key project, there was a staffing shortage that
reduced mentoring time, the new hire had a documented medical or family
emergency), and the next formal review date. HR-208 is placed in the new
hire's personnel file in the CHRO's office and becomes the official
record of onboarding completion or extension. The new hire receives a
copy for their records.

**Green outcome: Onboarding complete.** The standard and most common
outcome is a green assessment. The hire is now a full, confirmed
employee by all measures: compensation is finalized, title is confirmed,
benefits are active, and they are treated as a full peer in scheduling,
communication, and expectations. The manager no longer fills out a
checkpoint form and the new hire exits the onboarding program. For
employees hired through employee referrals, the referral bonus under the
Recruiting and Interview Policy is processed by HR and Payroll. Formal
performance reviews begin at the next annual review cycle (typically at
six months or one year from start, depending on the review schedule). A
new hire who is green at 90 days is treated like any other employee in
terms of performance management going forward, though managers typically
continue weekly one-to-ones for some time to maintain mentorship.

**Yellow outcome: Extended onboarding.** A yellow outcome means
readiness is not yet certain and an extension is warranted. This is not
a negative or a failure; it is a sign that more time or a shift in focus
will get the new hire to green. A yellow assessment might reflect: the
new hire is strong culturally and has shown learning but needs
additional hands-on time with a critical system before owning it
independently; the new hire is technically solid but has not yet bonded
with the team; the new hire is performing well but in a role that is new
to them and deserves another month to demonstrate sustained performance;
or the new hire hit an unexpected blocker (onboarding for a visa
sponsorship took longer than expected, a key peer who was supposed to
mentor was out on extended leave, an urgent project consumed mentoring
time). An extension plan specifies clear, measurable criteria for
readiness by a revised checkpoint date (usually 30 days hence, at Day
120, or in rare cases up to Day 150). The criteria are concrete: "The
engineer will own and ship an authentication refactoring project
independently, with no blocking issues"; "The sales rep will have closed
at least one opportunity and demonstrated discovery and proposal skills
on three deals"; "The support specialist will achieve 95% first-contact
resolution rate and a customer satisfaction score of 8 or above for 30
consecutive days." The manager, the hire, and HR all agree to the plan
and sign off on it. The peer buddy may continue for an additional 30 to
60 days if that supports the extension plan. Any referral bonus under the
Recruiting and Interview Policy is held and will be paid on the revised
checkpoint date or shortly thereafter, contingent on the extension plan
criteria being met. Extension plans are approved by the CHRO's
office and are placed in the hire's personnel file. A second checkpoint
conversation happens at the revised extension date to confirm readiness.
If at the end of the extension (typically Day 120 or Day 150), the
employee has met the criteria, their status is updated to green and they
enter the standard review cycle. If they have not met the criteria, a
second extension might be offered (very rare, typically no more than 60
days total extension time), or the conversation shifts to a Performance
Improvement Plan or a discussion about alternative roles. The principle
is that everyone deserves a fair onboarding period and clear
expectations, and Northwind is willing to extend that period if the gaps
are fixable. But Northwind is also clear about holding a standard once
the employee is confirmed.

**Red outcome: Performance or role fit concern.** A red outcome is rare
and reflects either a performance concern (the new hire is capable but
not executing at the standard expected for the role) or a role fit
concern (the new hire's skills, interests, or work style do not align
with the role or the team's needs). A red outcome requires a
conversation with the new hire, HR, and the department VP or head about
next steps. Options include: reassignment to a different role if the
issue is primarily a fit concern (a new engineer might move from backend
to infrastructure support, for example); a Performance Improvement Plan
(PIP) if the issue is performance and the fix is achievable; or, in rare
cases, a decision to end employment. Red outcomes are unusual if the
manager has been engaged through Week One and the 30-day check-in,
because they mean either the manager did not escalate warning signs from
earlier checkpoints or there was a fundamental misalignment that the
hiring process should have caught.

**Connection to the referral program.** The employee referral bonus under
the Recruiting and Interview Policy is paid to any employee who referred
the new hire, assuming the new hire reaches a green or yellow checkpoint
at 90 days (or Day 30 for internal transfers). The bonus is processed by
Payroll in coordination with HR. If a new hire's checkpoint is red, the
referral bonus provisions are forfeited, on the logic that the program
aims to reward employees for bringing in strong hires who stay and
contribute. This is explained to new hires and their referrers at the
start of employment (during onboarding orientation for the new hire and
in the referral program materials for the referrer). If a referred
employee reaches a yellow checkpoint, the referrer is entitled to the
referral bonus contingent on the extension plan; if the employee is later
terminated during the yellow extension, the referrer's status depends on
the specific terms outlined in the Recruiting and Interview Policy. If a
referred employee is separated before Day 90 (voluntary or involuntary),
the referral bonus is forfeited and is not owed.

**Equipment return and HR-114 records.** The 90-day checkpoint is also
the formal completion of the equipment provisioning loop started in Week
One with HR-114. If a new hire is separated before Day 90 (voluntary
departure, termination, or any other reason), the checkpoint is not
completed, and the employee is not formally confirmed. Equipment collected
at separation is checked against the HR-114 form signed at the start of
employment to ensure all items are returned in condition. An employee
separated after Day 90 has their final status and compensation handled in
accordance with company policy. Equipment return procedures are outlined in
the Equipment Return Policy; all hardware must be returned and verified by
IT. Damage determinations and any corresponding employee deductions are
subject to the Equipment Return Policy and applicable labor law.
Data wiping and secure deletion of any local copies of customer data are
IT's responsibility and are confirmed before equipment is recycled or
reissued. If HR-114 was never signed at the start of employment (a rare
administrative failure), this becomes a problem at the checkpoint or at
separation: without the signed form, IT and HR cannot definitively
establish what equipment was issued, in what condition, or the
employee's acknowledgment of responsibility. In such cases, HR obtains a
retroactive signature or a written acknowledgment from the employee at
the time of discovery, ideally within two business days. Failure to sign
HR-114 or obtain a retroactive acknowledgment creates a liability gap
for Northwind and means the employee cannot access production systems
until the matter is resolved.

**Connection to the performance management cycle.** The 90-day
checkpoint is administratively distinct from the annual or semi-annual
performance review cycle. An employee who is green at 90 days and later
receives a needs- improvement rating at their six-month informal review
or annual performance review is not automatically at risk of separation
or remediation. Ongoing performance management follows separate
protocols and involves regular manager feedback, coaching, and formal
Performance Improvement Plans if a performance gap emerges. The
checkpoint confirms that onboarding is complete and the hire
demonstrated basic competence and cultural fit. It does not commit
Northwind to continued employment at a specific performance level, and
it does not restrict the manager's ability to provide corrective
feedback or to initiate performance management later. Conversely, an
employee who receives strong performance ratings at six months or one
year has already moved well beyond onboarding and is competing
successfully at full level, and any future performance concerns would be
handled through the standard management and review process, not as an
onboarding issue.
