# Enterprise Deal Desk Playbook

## What the Deal Desk Does

The Deal Desk sits between Sales and Finance to ensure every enterprise
agreement reflects our terms, pricing, and risk posture. We are the
gatekeepers of revenue quality, not speed. Our job is to approve
quotes, validate discounts, screen non-standard terms before legal sees
them, and hand off complete order forms to Onboarding. We do not close
deals; we make sure closed deals are safe to book. Every agreement that
crosses our desk will be executed against, and we want it to be written
so carefully that disputes are rare and our margin is real.

When to involve the Deal Desk depends on size and complexity. Any
opportunity above $100k ACV requires our review before quoting.
Discount approval flows through us for anything above our approver's
limit. Non-standard payment terms, liability caps, security exceptions,
data residency requirements, custom SLAs, or termination for
convenience clauses all route to Deal Desk first. If the customer asks
for something outside our standard contract, we are the first filter.
Small deals under $50k with standard terms and no exceptions can skip
the desk entirely, but the account owner must confirm in writing that
no deal modifications are planned. A deal that slips through the desk
because Sales believed it was small, then turns out to have custom
liability language, puts us at risk. So the rule is simple: if you are
unsure, ask the desk. We would rather spend 15 minutes clarifying that
a $45k deal is truly standard than miss a non-standard term that costs
us a lawsuit.

We do not write contracts or negotiate terms with customers. That is
legal's job after we approve the business shape. We do not do financial
due diligence or check credit worthiness, that is a finance function.
We are the translation layer: Sales asks "can we do X", we check policy
and risk, and we say yes, no, or "yes but Finance or Legal must sign
off first". We unblock good deals and reject bad ones quickly. We also
document why we said yes or no, so that three months from now, when the
deal is closed and a question comes up, we can point to a decision memo
and explain the logic. The desk exists partly to move deals through,
but just as much to leave a record of what we decided and why, so that
the company learns from its own history.

Our turnaround commitment is four business days for standard intake and
approval. If you submit a quote request on a Monday morning, you have
an answer by Friday close of business. For deals above $250k annual
contract value that route through our fast-track queue DD-QUEUE-2, the
commitment is two business days instead. That two-day window is real
only if your submission is complete: pricing clearly stated, all
non-standard terms listed, discount justification included, and
customer profile filled in. If we have to ask for clarification, the
clock restarts when we get it. So a deal that arrives with half the
information missing will take four days to get a first response saying
"here is what we need", then four more days once you provide it. Budget
for that timeline. A complete submission is faster. We also track our
own turnaround in a dashboard, and we report it monthly to the Sales
leadership and to Finance. If we slip, we escalate internally. But if
Sales misses our intake rules or sends half a deal, that delay is on
Sales, not on us.

Our intake rule is simple. Submit via the Deal Desk intake form at
deals.northwind.example, not email. The form captures: deal name,
customer name, customer annual revenue (to size them), opportunity ACV,
discount percentage if any, discount reason (competitive, volume, land,
multi-product), list of any non-standard terms requested, and who the
sales sponsor is. If you email a deal request, we will not look at it
until you submit the form. We do this because email gets lost, forms
ensure we have everything we need, and a paper trail helps when the CFO
asks later why we approved a $500k deal at 35% off. The form also feeds
our deal database, so we can run reports on discount trends, on which
customers are asking for which custom terms, on turnaround times, and
on the financial impact of our approvals. Email breaks that pipeline.
Use the form.

When a deal arrives incomplete, we send back a checklist of missing
items rather than a vague rejection. Sales can then gather what we
need and resubmit. Resubmission resets the clock, so if your initial
submission is missing discount justification, customer profile data, or
a list of non-standard terms, expect the intake-to-decision cycle to
take the full four business days (or two for the fast track) from the
date we receive your complete resubmission. The intent is not to punish
incomplete submissions but to ensure that our decision is based on
complete information. A decision made on partial data is often wrong,
and a wrong decision early in a deal's cycle causes rework downstream.
Completeness at intake saves time overall.

The Deal Desk also maintains a reference library of common deal scenarios
and approved exceptions that Sales can reference before submitting. If you
are asking for a term that we have approved many times before, check the
library first; the approval pathway may be faster. The library documents
precedent approvals for custom SLAs, discount exceptions, payment term
variations, and liability cap adjustments. It also tracks which customer
segments have negotiated which terms historically, so Sales can understand
what a similar customer has received in the past. This library is updated
quarterly and is the authoritative reference for "has Northwind done this
before". If your deal resembles a precedent in the library, we can often
fast-track approval because the commercial risk is already understood and
the legal risk has been vetted. If your deal is genuinely novel, expect
standard turnaround. Tracking precedent also helps Finance understand
whether a discount or term is becoming standard by customer type; if
half of our healthcare customers now get Net 45 when we used to offer
Net 30, Finance needs to know that the market is shifting.

Most deals will be approved at the Deal Desk level. Anything under
$100k with standard terms gets a yes within days. Deals from $100k to
$250k with no discount or a standard discount inside the Discount
Approval Policy's sales-manager band, and no term changes also get a
yes from us. The friction starts above $250k or with non-standard
terms. At that
point, Finance and Legal enter the loop, and timing extends. But we
never skip those gates. A $500k deal with custom liability language
that goes to contract without Finance review is the kind of mistake
that costs us money in a dispute five years later. We have seen
companies get sued on contracts nobody at the finance table had read,
and the defense was basically "we did not know we said that". We are
not that company.

The Deal Desk also owns the quote validity rule. Every quote we issue
carries the validity period set by the Proposal and Quote Process,
printed on the PDF. If the customer does not sign within that period, we
re-issue. This is not a guideline, it is a policy enforced by our
quoting system. After a quote ages beyond validity, material conditions
have changed: your price list may have, the customer's seat count may
have shifted, licensing terms may have adjusted based on our annual
update cycle. A quote older than its stated validity gets re-validated
and re-priced. Sales sometimes pushes back on this, saying the customer
verbally committed but needs three months to get a PO through their
process. Our answer is the same: re-issue the quote if it is stale. The
customer can sign it whenever they want, but we will not honor a quote
we issued in April when they come back in July. That rule exists because
selling at May prices in August is revenue risk, and because customers
who take three months to execute are often using our quote as an option,
shopping us against a competitor. Refreshing the quote flushes out the
seriousness of their intent. It also means that the customer is getting
the current list price and current discount, not a locked-in price from
months ago that may now be below our margin floor. We reset quotes not
to punish customers but to keep our own house clean.

The desk also manages escalations. Sales gets into a jam where they
promised something they should not have, or they are stuck between a
customer demand and a policy, or they need to know whether an exception
is possible. They call the desk. We listen, we think, and we either
unblock them quickly or we send them to Finance or Legal with a
specific question. We try to give Sales a yes or no within a few hours,
not days. If we need to go up to the CFO or to a lawyer, we do it the
same day. Sales should never feel like the desk is slowing down a deal
because we are overcautious. We are careful, but we are also fast.

## Building an Enterprise Quote

An enterprise quote is not a price estimate. It is a commercial
document that becomes the basis for the order form and the contract.
Every line item, every term, every price, every discount, every
milestone and every limitation must be true in the quote or the
contract will contradict it later, and that contradiction will cost
credibility and sometimes money. We build quotes to be signed, not to
be negotiated down again later. Once a customer has a quote from us,
they have a snapshot of what we are selling and what it costs.
Precision matters because the quote becomes the reference point for
every conversation that follows.

The building blocks are line items. Each line item is a product tier,
the annual cost, the seat count or unit count that tier represents, and
the term length. We have standard tiers across our three main products.
Northwind Core, our flagship SaaS platform, comes in three tiers:
Starter (up to 50 seats), Pro (51 to 250 seats), and Enterprise. Atlas,
our data integration product, comes in three tiers: Standard,
Professional, and Enterprise. Meridian, our customer portal, comes in
two tiers: Standard (up to 10 external admins) and Professional (11 to
100 external admins). Enterprises often buy across all three, so a
quote might have a line for Northwind Core at the Enterprise tier, a
line for Atlas Professional, and a line for Meridian. That mix is
common. Each product line has its own scope, its own pricing, and its
own term, but all three lines go on one quote and one order form, and
the customer gets one bill.

When you quote a customer, you use our published SKU. For Northwind
Core Enterprise at the 500-seat tier, the SKU is NW-CORE-ENT-500. For
Atlas Professional at the 100 pipeline tier, the SKU is
NW-ATLAS-PRO-100. For Meridian customer portal at the 25 external admin
tier, the SKU is NW-MER-PORT-25. These are the SKUs that appear on the
order form and on the invoice. You do not invent a new SKU for this
customer, you do not hyphenate differently, you do not add a suffix.
The SKU is the SKU. These standardized SKUs exist for a reason: when we
send the order form to Accounting, they need to know exactly what we
sold. When the customer renews, we need to know what we were selling
them before, so we can offer a renewal at the same tier or a bump. When
support configures their account, they need to know the SKU so they
provision the right seat count or admin tier. A bespoke SKU breaks all
of that. When a customer asks for a custom tier that does not fit our
standard buckets (say, Core at 375 seats when we sell 250 or 500), you
do not create a bespoke SKU. Instead, you propose the next tier up. If
they want 375 seats and the jump from 250 to 500 is too steep on price,
that is a negotiation for a deeper discount, not a new product tier. We
refuse bespoke SKUs because they create downstream confusion: billing
does not know how to true-up a custom tier, support does not know what
seats are included, renewal is a mess because the customer thinks their
tier is unique when every tier is standard. Standard tiers, standard
SKUs. That is non-negotiable. If a customer is so large or so important
that we want to make an exception, that decision comes from Dana Okafor
(CEO) or Victor Lindqvist (COO), not from Sales or the Deal Desk.

Pricing for each line item is annual, and it is list price minus
discount if any. List prices are maintained by Product and Finance and
are the single source of truth. You do not negotiate a new list price
with a customer. You negotiate a discount. If you are quoting Northwind
Core Enterprise 500 seats at $180,000 per year list, and the customer
needs $150,000 to get approval from their finance team, you offer a
16.7% discount, bringing the total to $150,000. You do not invent a new
list price that happens to be $150,000. That matters because list price
is what goes into renewal notices, price increase letters, and our
financial forecasts. Discounts are temporary. They apply to this deal,
this year. List prices are the baseline, the reference point, and the
starting line for every conversation. An ACV of $500k at a 20% discount
means the customer is paying $400k, but our forecast shows $500k, and
our discount approval process knows a $100k concession was made. That
transparency helps Finance understand our margin, and helps Sales
leadership understand when they are negotiating too hard.

Many enterprise deals have a ramp schedule. The customer signs today
but does not roll out to full seats until Month 6. Year 1 might be 30%
capacity, Year 2 might be 60%, Year 3 full. When you quote a ramp, the
quote shows the Year 1 cost, the Year 2 cost, and Year 3 cost. Each
year's cost is based on the seat tier and list price for that tier. Do
not apply a discount to each year individually and then stack
discounts. Apply the discount once to the ACV (annual contract value),
then show the ramped cost for each year. If the customer is ramping
Northwind Core Enterprise 500 from 30% to 100% over three years at a
15% discount, Year 1 is $150,000 (30% of the discounted ACV), Year 2 is
$300,000 (60% of the discounted ACV), Year 3 is $500,000 (the full
discounted ACV). The math is clean, the discount is obvious and applied
once, and when Year 3 comes and they want to renew, we know exactly
what they are paying. Ramps also align our cash collection to the
customer's deployment plan, so we are not invoicing for seats they have
not activated yet. That also reduces support burden, because the
customer cannot activate seats they have not paid for.

True-up is the correction that happens when reality does not match the
quote. A customer ramped to 30% in Year 1 but hit 50% actual usage by
Month 9. At the annual true-up, they owe the difference between what
they paid (30% of the year's cost) and what they used (50% of the
year's cost). True-up is not a penalty, it is an adjustment. It is how
we stay aligned with actual consumption. When you quote a deal with a
ramp, mention in the quote that annual true-ups will apply. The
customer should not be surprised at Year 1 renewal that they owe more
because they grew. The renewal team will reference that language, and
the conversation becomes "you used more, so you pay a bit more", not
"we are charging you for upgrades you never asked for". True-ups also
allow us to price a ramp aggressively, because we know we will capture
upside as the customer scales up. The customer benefits from a lower
Year 1 cost, and we benefit from the true-up when they exceed their
ramped allocation.

Overages are customers who exceed their licensed tier. If they bought
Northwind Core at 250 seats but have 270 seats active in November,
those 20 overage seats are billed at 150% of the per-seat cost of their
license tier. You do not force them back down to 250. You bill the
overage at the end of the month. This is in our standard terms, and it
applies unless the customer negotiates a ceiling (like, "our license
covers up to 250 seats but anything over goes on the next tier at full
price"). Overages keep customers from needing to buy a whole new tier
just to stay legal. If a customer grows from 250 to 251 seats, they do
not want to buy the 500-seat tier. They want to pay a little bit extra
for one seat. Overages are humane and they are profitable, because the
overage rate is higher than the per-seat rate of their current tier.

Multi-year deals sometimes include uplift. The customer commits to year
2 and year 3, and in exchange for that upfront commitment, you give
them a slight discount on the year 2 and 3 cost. Do not stack
multi-year uplift on top of volume discount. You give them one or the
other. A volume discount applies because they are a large customer
today. A multi-year uplift applies because they are committing to a
longer term. Both together are double-dipping and they destroy margin.
Pick the better one for the customer or offer the worse one if you are
the one asking for the longer commitment. If the customer comes to us
and says "we will sign a three-year deal if you give us a better
price", that is multi-year uplift, and we can offer it. If the customer
comes to us because they are a $2M ACV customer and they want a volume
discount, we offer that instead. Not both. When you are structuring a
multi-year deal, be clear about which years the uplift applies to.
Usually it is year 2 and 3. Year 1 is at the negotiated price. Year 2
and 3 get the uplift, bringing the per-seat cost down slightly. That
structure keeps incentives aligned: the customer gets an upgrade reward
for committing early.

The 30-day quote validity applies to every quote we issue. Once the
quote PDF is generated and sent to the customer, it is valid for 30
calendar days from the date printed on the PDF. If a customer receives
the quote on January 5 and signs on February 10, the quote is stale,
and we will not honor it. The customer can request a refreshed quote
with today's terms. This policy is in our standard language, and
customers know it. Some will push back, saying they need more time
because their CFO is traveling or their procurement process is slow.
Our answer is still 30 days. If they want the price to stay locked
longer, that is not our problem to solve. If the customer insists they
were verbally promised a 60-day window, that is a red flag that Sales
made a promise we did not authorize. Escalate to your sales leader and
the Deal Desk immediately. We do not honor verbal price locks. A quote
is a quote, and a quote is 30 days. After 30 days, we refresh it.

## Non-Standard Terms and How to Get Them Approved

Nearly every large customer asks for something outside our standard
contract. They want Net 45 or Net 60 instead of Net 30. They want
unlimited liability or a cap that is higher than our standard 12-month
fees. They want a custom SLA that guarantees 99.99% uptime instead of
our standard 99.5%. They ask for termination for convenience so they
can cancel without cause. They ask for their data to be stored in a
specific region. They send us a 40-page security questionnaire and ask
us to remediate every finding. They want master service agreements with
addenda and exhibits. They want us to accept their template instead of
ours. These are not unusual requests. But they are not standard either,
and they do not get approved because the customer asks for them. Every
non-standard term requires explicit approval from Finance, Legal, or
both, and the approval must happen before the customer is promised
anything. A customer who believes a term is approved when it actually
still needs legal review is a customer who will be angry when we
finally get back with notes or a rejection.

Payment terms are the most common negotiation. Our standard is Net 30:
invoice on signature, payment due 30 days later. Most US customers
accept this happily. International customers often want Net 45 or Net
60, especially if they have centralized procurement or they are in
Europe and their regulatory process is slow. You can offer Net 45
without approval. It costs a bit more cash float, but not enough to
break Finance. Net 60 requires Finance (Marcus Webb, CFO, or the
Finance Operations Manager) to sign off, because holding invoices for
60 days affects our cash flow forecasts and our ability to make payroll
if we have a slow collection quarter. Anything longer than 60 days
needs the CFO personally and almost never gets approved. You do not
offer Net 90 or Net 120. If a customer asks for it, the answer is no,
or if they are a big enough deal and we have reason to believe they are
good for it, "we can offer Net 60, and we will need 50% upfront to
offset the holdback". That upfront payment protects us, because we have
half the cash in hand immediately. We do not do payment term discounts.
You do not give them a 5% reduction because they want Net 60. The
customer pays the same amount, they just pay it later. Our price is our
price; payment terms are a separate lever.

Liability caps are the second most common negotiation. Our standard
contract caps our liability at 12 months of fees paid, and excludes
liability for indirect, consequential, or incidental damages. That
12-month cap applies to everything: performance failures, security
issues, data loss, service outages. Large customers push back, saying
12 months is too low for a mission-critical system, or they want
unlimited liability for data breaches. Uncapped liability is a
non-starter. We will not agree to it. We are not big enough or well
enough insured to carry unlimited exposure for any product. A cap
higher than 12 months requires Amelia Brooks, General Counsel, or her
team to review and approve. A cap of 24 months of fees is often
acceptable if the customer is a strong negotiator, the deal is large,
and they are willing to negotiate down from a higher ask. A cap of 36
months is rarely approved without an escalation to Amelia and the CFO.
Do not offer 36 months without checking with both. Data breaches are
covered by our cyber insurance, and Priya Raman (CISO) has authority
over any language that touches data breach liability. If the customer
wants unlimited liability for data breaches, we escalate that to Priya
and Amelia together, and the answer is usually no, unless we are
acquiring a specific customer and it is a strategic imperative. The
customer does not get uncapped liability. But we might agree to a
modest uplift (say, 24 months instead of 12) if we carry cyber
insurance that covers the difference and Priya signs off on the
language.

Custom SLAs are requests to guarantee higher uptime or faster response
times than our standard support SLAs. Our standard uptime SLA is 99.5%,
which means 3.6 hours of allowed downtime per year. Our standard
support response time is 1 hour for P1 issues and 4 hours for P2. A
customer asking for 99.9% or 99.99% uptime is asking us to engineer to
a higher bar. 99.9% uptime means 52 minutes allowed per year. 99.99%
means 5 minutes allowed per year. That is possible, but it costs money
and it means Ravi Iyer (CTO) and the engineering team need to sign off
that the infrastructure can support it, and that ongoing monitoring and
escalation protocols are in place. You do not promise a custom SLA
without engineering's approval. If a customer needs higher uptime, you
route the request to Ravi with the opportunity size and timeline, and
you wait for his team to say yes or no. If yes, the SLA goes in the
contract with specific downtime windows and maintenance windows and
notice periods are the ones published in the Scheduled Maintenance
Policy, along with credits for missed targets. If no, you tell the customer we cannot deliver it,
or we can deliver it for an additional fee to offset the engineering
cost. Do not tell the customer we will "look into it" and then
disappear. Either we can do it, we cannot do it, or we can do it for
more money. Any of those is better than a vague maybe.

Security questionnaires are routine for large customers, but they are
not a Done item until Legal and Security review them. When a customer
sends you a security questionnaire, you acknowledge receipt within one
business day, you give them a timeline (typically 10 business days,
sometimes 15 for a complex form), and you route it to Priya Raman
(CISO) and the legal team. You do not answer the questionnaire
yourself. Priya's team reviews it, flags any questions that ask for
things we do not have (like, FIPS 140-2 compliance if we do not have
it), and pushes back on unreasonable demands (like, "we need a
dedicated instance just for us" or "we need you to store backups in our
data center"). Once Priya approves the responses, Legal reviews it to
make sure nothing in our answers creates a new contractual obligation
we cannot meet. Then the Deal Desk sends the completed form back to the
customer. This usually takes two to three weeks, sometimes more if it
is a 60-page questionnaire from a large bank with 200 questions. Budget
for it. Do not tell the customer you will send it back "next week" if
you know Priya will need 10 days. Give them a realistic timeline.

Termination for convenience is a clause that says the customer can
cancel at any time for any reason, without cause, and without penalty.
Our standard contract is 12-month term with auto-renewal, and a 90-day
notice requirement to prevent renewal. The customer has to stay for 12
months. If they want to leave early, they are in breach. Termination
for convenience flips that: they can leave whenever they want,
sometimes on 30 days notice, sometimes immediately. We rarely agree to
this, because it kills the revenue certainty of a 12-month contract.
You can close a $500k deal at list price with termination for
convenience, and the customer can cancel in Month 2 and leave you with
$500k of expected revenue that never shows up. If the customer insists
on it, the price goes up 20-25% to offset the risk that they will
cancel early. If you want to offer termination for convenience, flag it
with Marcus Webb (CFO) and Amelia Brooks (General Counsel) before you
propose it to the customer. That is not a deal desk decision; it is a
business decision above us. If Finance says yes and wants to price for
the risk, we run the numbers and we approve it. If Finance says no, we
go back and tell the customer no.

Data residency and localization requests are increasing, because
customers have regulatory or contractual obligations to keep data in a
specific region. We run workloads in the US, EU, and APAC regions, with
compliance certifications for most of them. If a customer wants all
their data in the EU and we have EU infrastructure, that is easy. We
note it in the order form and hand it to Onboarding. If the customer
wants data in a region we do not serve (like, India, or mainland
China), that is not easy. It may be possible, but it requires
infrastructure decisions and potentially additional cost to set up new
facilities or partner with a regional vendor. You do not promise data
residency we do not have. Route it to Victor Lindqvist (COO) or Ravi
Iyer (CTO) to figure out feasibility and cost. If we can do it, we
charge for it. If we cannot do it, we say no. Some customers will walk
away. That is OK. Better to lose a deal than to promise data residency
we cannot deliver and then scramble when they ask us to prove it during
onboarding.

The sequencing for non-standard term approval is strict: Deal Desk
first, Finance second, Legal third. You route the request to Deal Desk
(that is you), we screen it, we check policy, and we flag what needs
Finance and Legal input. Then the request goes to Finance (Marcus Webb
or the Finance Operations Manager). Finance validates the commercial
shape: does the discount make sense for the customer size, does the
payment term affect our cash flow forecast, does the deal pencil
against the cost of goods sold, does the margin meet our floor. Then it
goes to Legal (Amelia Brooks or her team), and Legal validates the
contract language, the liability terms, the regulatory compliance, the
dispute resolution language, and the overall risk posture. You do not
send a non-standard term request to Legal before Finance approves. You
do not tell the customer a term is approved before Legal signs off. The
sequence is Deal Desk, Finance, Legal. That order exists because
Finance can kill a deal on commercial grounds (say, the discount is too
steep for the margin we need), and if Finance kills it, Legal never
sees it, and we save everyone time. And Legal should be the last gate
because a term that Finance approved might still be legally risky or
expose us to a new type of claim.

The cardinal rule is this: no term is promised to the customer before
it is approved. You do not tell the customer "we will give you Net 60",
"we will cap liability at 24 months", or "we will guarantee 99.9%
uptime" until Finance and Legal have signed off in writing. If the
customer asks in a call and you are not 100% sure, you say "that is a
good question, and I will check with our finance and legal teams and
get back to you by Thursday". You do not say "yes, I think we can do
that". Customers remember yes. They forget "I will check". If you say
yes and then have to come back three weeks later and say "we actually
cannot do that", the deal stalls, trust erodes, and the customer starts
comparing us to a competitor who already said yes. Slow is better than
wrong. It is also better for the customer: they would rather hear "that
is complex, let us get back to you" than get excited about a term that
we cannot deliver.

## Handoff to Onboarding

Once the order form is countersigned by the customer and the CEO (for
deals over $1M) or the Finance team (for deals $250k to $1M), or the
sales leader (for deals under $250k), the Deal Desk closes the approval
and hands the deal to Onboarding. Onboarding is Victor Lindqvist (COO)
and his team. They own the customer kickoff, the implementation
timeline, the success metrics, and the handoff to Support. They get a
handoff document that includes everything Onboarding and Finance need
to deliver on the deal. This is not an email. This is a structured
handoff form filled out in our deal management system, and both the
Deal Desk and Sales sign it. The handoff is the transfer of
accountability from Sales to Delivery. Once Onboarding receives it and
reviews it, they own the customer success.

The handoff includes the deal name, customer name, deal ACV, the
effective date of the contract (when the customer begins paying), the
term length (usually 12 months, sometimes 24 or 36), the renewal date
(when we send a renewal notice, usually 90 days before contract end),
and the auto-renewal setting (yes, always yes unless the customer
specifically negotiated no auto-renewal). It includes every line item
and SKU from the quote, the seat count or unit count for each, and any
ramps or true-up provisions. It includes the discount, the discount
reason (competitive, land, multi-product, volume), and the dollar
amount of the discount so Accounting knows revenue is recognized at the
discounted price, not list. It includes the customer's billing contact
and technical contact, and the preferred language for support
communications if it is not English.

It includes any non-standard term, every single one: Net 45 instead of
Net 30, unlimited liability for data breaches, custom SLA with 99.9%
uptime guarantee, data residency in the EU, termination for convenience
with 30-day notice, anything that deviates from standard. It includes
the Security Questionnaire approval status and any conditions (like,
SOC 2 attestation required by Month 3, or data retention must be 90
days). It includes the names and emails of the deal sponsor (Sales),
the finance approver, the legal approver, and the customer's main
contact. It includes any custom integrations that were promised, any
professional services, any training, any pilot period, and the timeline
for each. If the customer is piloting for 30 days before going live,
that goes in the handoff. If the customer is getting a free month of
service while they migrate data, that goes in the handoff.

It also includes the customer's industry, their size, their use case,
and their success criteria. This is context for Onboarding, so that the
implementation team understands what winning looks like for this
customer. If the customer is a financial services firm and they are
using Northwind Core to automate compliance reporting, Onboarding needs
to know that. If the customer is a healthcare company and data privacy

Support understand what the customer cares about most.

The kickoff timing is critical. Onboarding should not learn about a
deal from the order form showing up in their email inbox. They should
hear about it at least one week before the contract is signed. The deal
sponsor (your Sales contact) emails the Deal Desk and Onboarding
together with a note saying "this deal is approaching signature,
kickoff scheduled for August 15". That email warns Onboarding that they
are about to get an inbound deal and gives them time to check calendar,
allocate resources, and reach out to the customer to schedule a kickoff
call. A good Sales contact will loop Onboarding in much earlier, at the
proposal stage, so they can give input on feasibility or timeline.
Onboarding's SLA is a kickoff call within five business days of the
signed order form. If the order form comes to them cold with no
warning, they will miss that SLA and the customer will be frustrated.
So Sales tells us, we tell Onboarding, Onboarding plans. The day the
order form is signed, it goes to Finance first, then to the Deal Desk,
and the Deal Desk sends the handoff form to Onboarding within 24 hours.

The commitments recorded in the handoff are everything Onboarding and
the delivery team are promising to the customer. If we quoted the
customer a data migration from their old system, with a specific
timeline and the customer's data ready on Day 1, and a support engineer
assigned as a primary contact, those are all commitments. The delivery
team needs to know them before they start. If the quote promised custom
integrations with the customer's warehouse system, someone in
Onboarding or Professional Services needs to be assigned to that
project before kickoff. If the quote said "your Meridian implementation
will be complete by June 30", that date goes in the handoff, and the
project manager budgets to that date. If the quote promised a training
program for 50 end users, that goes in the handoff and Finance approves
the cost. The customer expects it, and if Onboarding does not know it
was promised, they will not budget resources for it, and the customer
will feel abandoned.

We do not hand off surprises. If the contract says the customer gets a
custom SLA with a guaranteed response time of 30 minutes for P1 issues,
Onboarding needs to know that because Support (Nadia Petrov, VP
Customer Support) will need to staff to that level and may need to
escalate how fast they respond on P1 for this account. If the contract
says the customer can terminate for convenience with 30 days notice,
the delivery team needs to know that this is a month-to-month deal, not
a 12-month commitment, and they should not be planning a 12-month
onboarding roadmap with a big implementation that takes six months to
pay back. If the contract says data must be stored in the EU,
Onboarding needs to know that the customer's environments are
non-standard and require special provisioning. If the customer paid at
a higher tier but negotiated a lower seat count, Onboarding needs to
know the seat count, not the tier. If the customer gets a discount on
Year 2 and Year 3 pending a milestone (like, "discount applies if you
achieve 80% adoption"), that goes in the handoff. You capture all of
that in the handoff form, and Onboarding reads it before they call the
customer for the first time. That is the deal desk's last job: make
sure the people who deliver the deal are not blindsided by a promise we
made to close it.

The handoff review is not a rubber stamp. Onboarding's project manager
reviews the handoff and either confirms receipt with no questions, or
escalates back to the Deal Desk with feasibility concerns before the
kickoff call. If Onboarding says "we cannot deliver the custom integration
in 30 days; it will take 60", the Deal Desk must escalate that back to
Sales and the customer immediately so that expectations can be reset and
the contract can be amended if needed. A handoff that says "let's try it
anyway" is a recipe for customer disappointment and a failed onboarding.
Onboarding's role is to say early if something is not feasible, not to
discover it six weeks into implementation.

The Deal Desk also flags any deals where terms conflict or where unspoken
dependencies exist between the quote, the contract, and the legal terms.
For example, if the quote promises implementation support but the contract
says implementation is out of scope, that conflict gets surfaced in the
handoff so the customer and Sales can clarify what was really promised.
If the customer negotiated a discount contingent on Year 2 adoption metrics,
but nobody has defined what those metrics are, the handoff notes the missing
definition and flags it for Sales to resolve with the customer before
Onboarding starts work. These conflicts are caught at handoff time, not
discovered months later when the customer asks "when does our Year 2 discount
kick in" and nobody can agree on whether they hit the milestone.

After the handoff is reviewed and Onboarding confirms receipt without
escalation, the deal is in their world. Finance starts billing setup and
invoicing. Support gets a ticket to provision the account and configure
the customer's seat count or admin tier. The project manager starts
building the implementation timeline based on the customer's availability
and the commitments we made. If there is a ramp, the project manager knows
when the customer reaches each milestone and budgets the expansion work.
If there is a custom integration, the engineering team gets assigned and
the schedule starts ticking. None of this happens on its own. It happens
because the handoff form was clear about what was promised and who is
responsible.

That is the deal desk's job: approve the terms, validate the pricing,
screen the risk, and hand off a complete picture to the people who will
execute. Every deal that rolls through the desk should be one that
Finance and Onboarding are ready to deliver. If we hand off a deal and
Onboarding says "we cannot do that", the deal is at risk. If Finance
sees the terms after signature and says "we should not have approved
this", the deal is at risk. We catch all of that before signature. We
are the guardrail, and we take that seriously.
